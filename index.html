Part 0

Orientation

What the words mean, before any technology. Four ideas that make everything after them cheaper to learn.

01

What "cloud native" actually means

Building software so that it expects to run on machines it does not own, and to survive those machines disappearing.

The phrase is not about being hosted in a cloud. Plenty of cloud-hosted software is not cloud native, and some cloud native software runs in a basement. It describes an assumption: that the underlying computers are disposable, interchangeable and occasionally on fire. Once you assume that, a set of design choices follows almost automatically, and those choices are what the term actually names.

Go deeper

The most-quoted definition centres on loosely coupled systems that are resilient, manageable and observable, combined with automation that lets engineers make high-impact changes frequently and predictably. The important word is *predictably*. Frequent change is easy; frequent change that does not increase risk is the hard part, and every technology in this guide exists to make some slice of that possible.

02

What the CNCF is, and what it is not

A neutral home that hosts cloud native projects. It does not build them.

The Cloud Native Computing Foundation is part of the Linux Foundation. Its job is custody, not engineering: it holds trademarks, runs governance, funds infrastructure and provides a level playing field so that no single vendor controls a project everyone depends on. The code is still written by maintainers, most of whom are paid by companies with their own interests.

CNCFCloud Native Computing Foundation

Part of the Linux Foundation. Hosts cloud native projects and holds their trademarks, but does not write their code.

Go deeper

This distinction explains things that are otherwise confusing. Why does the CNCF "host" Kubernetes rather than own its roadmap? Because hosting is the product. A company can bet its infrastructure on a CNCF project knowing that a single vendor cannot unilaterally relicense it, kill it, or hold it hostage. That guarantee, not any particular technology, is what the foundation sells.

03

How to read the landscape without drowning

It is a hierarchy pretending to be a wall. Read the category headings and ignore the logos.

landscape.cncf.io shows well over a thousand entries at once, which is why most people close it. But almost nothing in it is a thing you choose; the entries are grouped into categories, and the category is the real unit. You do not pick between two hundred tools, you work out which three categories your problem sits in and then compare a handful inside each.

Go deeper

A second thing hides in the noise: only a minority of landscape entries are actually CNCF projects. The rest are products and services from member companies that fit the category. Confusing "in the landscape" with "a CNCF project" is the single most common misreading, and it makes the ecosystem look far larger and more chaotic than it is.

Part 1

The unit: containers

Everything above this sits on one idea. Get this part properly and the rest stops being mysterious.

01

The problem before containers

"It works on my machine" is a deployment strategy, and a bad one.

Software depends on more than its own code: a language runtime, system libraries, configuration, a particular filesystem layout. Historically those lived on the server and were managed separately from the application, so the thing you tested and the thing you ran were never quite the same artefact. Most deployment failure was some version of that gap.

Go deeper

Virtual machines solved it by shipping the whole operating system, which worked but was heavy: gigabytes per artefact and a minute or more to boot. The insight behind containers is that you rarely need a second kernel, only a second *view* of the filesystem and process space. Keep the kernel, isolate everything above it, and the same guarantee costs megabytes and milliseconds.

Terms here

02

Image, container, registry

An image is the recipe, a container is the meal, a registry is the cookbook shelf.

These three get used interchangeably and should not be. An image is an immutable, layered filesystem plus metadata, built once and never changed. A container is a running process started from an image, with its own isolated view of the system. A registry is a server that stores and serves images. One image can start a thousand containers; none of them can alter the image.

Go deeper

The layering matters more than it first appears. Images are built in stacked read-only layers, each addressed by a content hash, and a running container adds one thin writable layer on top. Two images sharing a base layer store it once, and pulling an image you mostly already have transfers only the difference. Immutability plus content addressing is what makes container distribution fast enough to be practical.

Terms here

03

OCI: the specifications underneath

Three written standards that stop "container" meaning one vendor's product.

The Open Container Initiative publishes three specifications: the image spec (what an image is on disk), the runtime spec (how to run one) and the distribution spec (how registries serve them). Because these are written down and implemented by several independent tools, an image built with one tool runs under another and is stored by a third.

OCIOpen Container Initiative

Publishes the image, runtime and distribution specifications that make containers portable between vendors.

Go deeper

This is the first appearance of a pattern that repeats through the entire ecosystem, and it is worth naming now: specify the boundary, then let implementations compete behind it. OCI is why you can build with Podman or Buildah, store in Harbor or a cloud registry, and run under containerd or Podman, without any of those needing to know about the others.

04

Runtimes: high-level, low-level, sandboxed

Two layers of program, one that manages containers and one that actually starts them.

A low-level runtime does the narrow job the OCI runtime spec describes: take a bundle on disk, ask the kernel for the right namespaces and cgroups, start the process. runc is the reference implementation. A high-level runtime does everything around that: pull images, manage storage and networking, track lifecycle, and call a low-level runtime when it is time to actually run something. containerd and CRI-O are the common ones.

CRIContainer Runtime Interface

The API Kubernetes uses to talk to a container runtime. CRI-O is named after it. Covered properly in Part 3.

Go deeper

Sandboxed runtimes swap out the isolation model for workloads where kernel-level namespaces are not a strong enough boundary. gVisor puts a user-space kernel between the container and the host; Kata Containers gives each container a lightweight virtual machine. Both implement the same OCI runtime interface, so they slot in underneath an unchanged high-level runtime, which is the whole point of having written the spec down.

Terms here

05

Why "Docker" means four different things

A company, a CLI, a daemon and a file format all share one name, and people mix them up constantly.

Docker popularised containers and for years was the only way most people met them, so the name attached to the whole idea. Today Docker is a company; docker is a command-line tool; dockerd is a daemon that builds and runs containers; and a Dockerfile is a build format. Kubernetes never ran "Docker images" as a distinct thing, because there is no such thing: they are OCI images.

Go deeper

The "Kubernetes deprecates Docker" headlines in 2020 caused real panic and described something narrow. Kubernetes removed dockershim, a compatibility layer that let the kubelet talk to dockerd, because dockerd did not speak the interface Kubernetes wanted. Images kept working, because images were never the issue. The confusion was entirely about the name covering four things at once.

Part 2

Orchestration

One container is easy. The interesting problems all start at a few thousand.

01

What orchestration actually solves

Deciding which machine runs what, and noticing when that stops being true.

Given a fleet of machines and a set of workloads, something has to place each workload somewhere with enough capacity, restart it when it dies, move it when a machine fails, and let other workloads find it once it moves. Doing that by hand works up to about one machine. An orchestrator is the program that does it continuously.

Go deeper

The scale argument is the one usually given and it is the weaker one. The stronger argument is uniformity: once placement, restart, discovery and rollout are handled the same way for every workload, operational knowledge stops being per-application. That is why orchestration pays off well below the scale at which it is strictly necessary.

Terms here

02

Control plane and data plane

One set of components decides what should happen; another set carries it out.

The control plane holds the desired state and makes decisions: the API server takes every request, etcd stores the state, the scheduler chooses where new work goes, and controllers watch for drift. The data plane is the machines actually running your workloads, each with a kubelet taking instructions and a network proxy wiring up traffic.

Go deeper

The split explains the failure modes. If the control plane is down, running workloads keep running: they are just processes on a node, and nothing is checking on them. What stops is *change* — no new deployments, no rescheduling, no reaction to a node dying. Control plane outages are therefore rarely immediate outages, which is why they are sometimes noticed late.

Terms here

03

Declarative state and the reconciliation loop

You describe the end state you want; a loop keeps making reality match it, forever.

This is the single most important idea in the ecosystem. You do not issue commands like "start three copies". You write down that three copies should exist, and a controller compares that against what exists, then acts to close the gap. It never stops doing this. Kill a copy and the loop notices and replaces it, not because anything was watching for your action, but because the gap reappeared.

Go deeper

Understanding this converts most Kubernetes confusion into something predictable. It is why deleting a pod that a Deployment owns does not achieve anything; why editing a resource by hand gets silently reverted; and why the answer to "how do I make it do X" is almost always "change the declared state, not the running system". Every controller, every operator and all of GitOps is this loop with a different source of desired state.

Terms here

04

The object model you actually use

A handful of resource types cover most of what people deploy.

A Pod is one or more containers scheduled together sharing a network identity, and it is the smallest thing Kubernetes places. A ReplicaSet keeps N pods alive. A Deployment manages ReplicaSets to give you rolling updates and rollbacks. A Service gives a stable address to a changing set of pods. A Namespace partitions all of it for isolation and quota.

Go deeper

Almost nobody creates Pods directly, and the reason is the reconciliation loop: a bare Pod has no controller, so when it dies nothing recreates it. You create a Deployment so that something owns the gap. The same reasoning explains StatefulSets for workloads needing stable identity and storage, DaemonSets for one-per-node agents, and Jobs for work that is supposed to finish.

Terms here

05

CRDs and operators

Teach the cluster a new noun, then write a loop that knows how to run it.

A Custom Resource Definition adds a new resource type to the Kubernetes API, so the cluster can store and serve objects it was never built to understand. On its own that is just typed storage. An operator is the other half: a controller that watches those objects and runs the same reconciliation loop against them.

CRDCustom Resource Definition

Adds a new resource type to the Kubernetes API so the cluster can manage objects it was not built to understand.

Go deeper

The pattern encodes operational knowledge as software. A database operator does not merely install a database; it knows how to take backups, perform failover and run a version upgrade safely, and it does those things continuously rather than when someone remembers. That is why operators are the usual answer for stateful workloads, where the interesting work is all in the operations rather than the install.

Part 3

The interfaces

This is the acronym wall, and it is far smaller than it looks. Four letters-and-an-I names, one idea behind all of them.

01

Why any of these exist

Kubernetes refuses to pick a winner, so it defines a socket and lets vendors build plugs.

Kubernetes needs to run containers, connect them and give them storage. It could have shipped one implementation of each and been done. Instead it specifies a contract for each and ships none of them, so any vendor meeting the contract works without Kubernetes changing. Every acronym in this part is one of those contracts.

Go deeper

This is the same pattern OCI introduced one part ago, applied at a different boundary, and it is the mechanism by which the CNCF stays vendor-neutral in practice rather than only in its charter. It also explains why the ecosystem looks crowded: a well-specified interface invites many implementations, and all of them show up on the landscape.

Terms here

02

CRI, the Container Runtime Interface

How the kubelet asks something to run a container, without caring what that something is.

Every node runs a kubelet, and the kubelet does not start containers itself. It speaks CRI, a gRPC API, to whatever runtime is installed. containerd and CRI-O both implement it. Swap one for the other and the kubelet does not notice.

Go deeper

This is exactly what the "Kubernetes deprecates Docker" story was about. dockerd did not speak CRI, so Kubernetes shipped dockershim to translate, then removed that maintenance burden once CRI-native runtimes were widespread. Nothing about images changed. The interface, not the image format, was the thing being tidied up.

03

CNI, the Container Network Interface

How a container gets an IP address and a route to everything else.

When a pod is created something must give it a network identity and wire it into a network where every other pod is reachable. CNI is the contract for that: a plugin is invoked at pod creation and teardown, and it is responsible for the address and the plumbing. Calico, Cilium and Flannel are implementations.

CNIContainer Network Interface

The contract a networking plugin implements to give each pod an IP address and connectivity. Calico, Cilium and Flannel are implementations.

Go deeper

Kubernetes mandates the outcome and specifies almost nothing about the method: every pod gets its own IP, and all pods can reach each other without NAT. How you achieve that is entirely the plugin's business, which is why implementations differ so wildly. Some use overlay tunnels, some program the underlying network directly, and Cilium uses eBPF to do the work inside the kernel rather than through iptables rules.

04

CSI, the Container Storage Interface

How a workload gets a disk that outlives the container using it.

Containers are disposable, but data usually is not. CSI is the contract a storage system implements so that Kubernetes can provision a volume, attach it to whichever node needs it, and mount it into a pod, without Kubernetes containing any storage-vendor code.

CSIContainer Storage Interface

The contract a storage system implements so Kubernetes can provision, attach and mount volumes without vendor code in its core.

Go deeper

CSI was deliberately specified to be orchestrator-agnostic rather than Kubernetes-specific, which is why the same driver can serve other orchestrators. Before it, storage integrations lived in the Kubernetes tree itself, which meant a driver bug needed a Kubernetes release to fix. Moving them out is the same architectural move as CRI: shrink the core, specify the edge.

Terms here

05

CCM and Gateway API, the other two boundaries

One for talking to a cloud provider, one for getting traffic in from outside.

The Cloud Controller Manager is where cloud-specific logic lives: creating load balancers, labelling nodes with their region, handling instances that no longer exist. Gateway API is the newer specification for routing external traffic into a cluster, designed to replace Ingress with something expressive enough for real routing and role-aware enough for shared clusters.

CCMCloud Controller Manager

Holds the cloud-provider-specific logic (load balancers, node metadata) so the Kubernetes core stays provider-neutral.

Go deeper

Both are the same move again. CCM exists so that Kubernetes releases are not coupled to cloud provider release cycles. Gateway API exists because Ingress was underspecified, so every controller invented incompatible annotations to cover the gaps, which quietly destroyed the portability the abstraction was supposed to provide. The lesson repeats: an interface that is too thin gets extended in incompatible ways until it is no longer an interface.

Roles

Where each path actually ends

Twelve roles, what you learn in order, and the certification that attests to it. Every step links back into the path above. The verdict on each certification is honest, including the ones where the answer is do not bother.

Getting in at all

No cloud background, wants a first foothold.

You are not being hired as anything yet. The goal is enough vocabulary and hands-on familiarity that job descriptions stop looking like noise.

  1. Understand what "cloud native" is claiming
  2. Learn who the CNCF is and what it hosts
  3. Read the landscape as categories, not logos
  4. Get comfortable in a Linux shell. Not optional, and the most common gap
  5. Understand the problem containers solve
  6. Build and run an image by hand
  7. Run a single-node cluster locally, break it, fix it
Ends at KCNA Kubernetes and Cloud Native Associate or LFCA, Linux Foundation Certified IT Associate

Is it worth itKCNA is the one cert on this list that is genuinely useful without experience, because it certifies vocabulary and vocabulary is what you are missing. It is multiple choice and it will not get you hired on its own. If you cannot yet navigate a shell comfortably, do LFCA first and come back.

Kubernetes Administrator

Runs the clusters other people deploy to.

You own the cluster: upgrades, capacity, networking, storage, and the 3am call when scheduling stops working. The job is mostly diagnosis.

  1. Control plane vs data plane, and what fails when each dies
  2. Declarative state and the reconciliation loop
  3. The object model you will actually type
  4. How the kubelet starts a container
  5. How a pod gets an IP and reaches other pods
  6. How a workload gets a disk that outlives it
  7. RBAC, quotas and namespaces as real isolation
  8. etcd backup and restore, practised rather than read about
  9. Cluster upgrades and node drain without dropping traffic
Ends at CKA Certified Kubernetes Administrator

Is it worth itThis is the one hiring managers actually check, and unusually for a certification it is worth the money: it is a live terminal against broken clusters, not multiple choice, so passing it is evidence you can do the work rather than describe it. Do not sit it without months of real kubectl time.

Application Developer on Kubernetes

Ships services to a cluster someone else runs.

You write the application and everything needed to run it well: probes, config, resource limits, and enough observability to debug your own service at 2am.

  1. Why containers, from a developer's angle
  2. Images, layers and why build order matters
  3. Pods, Deployments and Services
  4. ConfigMaps, Secrets and the twelve-factor habit
  5. Liveness, readiness and startup probes, and how each is misused
  6. Resource requests and limits, and what OOMKilled really means
  7. Debugging a failing pod from logs and describe output alone
  8. Multi-container patterns, sidecars and init containers
Ends at CKAD Certified Kubernetes Application Developer

Is it worth itNarrower than CKA rather than easier. It skips cluster administration and goes deeper on the application layer. Worth it if you deploy to Kubernetes but will never run it; if you might end up owning the cluster, CKA is the better single purchase.

Kubernetes Security Specialist

Hardens clusters and the supply chain feeding them.

You reduce blast radius: what a compromised pod can reach, what gets admitted, what is signed, and what you can prove afterwards.

  1. Everything in the administrator path first. CKS requires an active CKA
  2. Trust boundaries: where the container isolation actually ends
  3. Sandboxed runtimes and when namespaces are not enough
  4. Admission control and policy as code (OPA, Kyverno)
  5. Supply chain: SBOMs, signing and verifying what you run
  6. Runtime detection and the audit trail you will need
  7. Network policy that actually denies by default
Ends at CKS Certified Kubernetes Security Specialist or KCSA, Kubernetes and Cloud Native Security Associate

Is it worth itCKS carries the strongest security signal in this catalogue and requires an active CKA to sit, so budget for both. KCSA is the multiple-choice associate version: useful as a stepping stone or if you work alongside security rather than in it, but it is not a substitute.

Linux / Systems Administrator

The layer everything above assumes you already know.

The unglamorous foundation. Almost every cloud native failure eventually bottoms out in a filesystem, a process, a permission or a network route.

  1. What bare metal and virtualisation actually buy you
  2. Namespaces and cgroups: the kernel features containers are made of
  3. systemd, journald and service lifecycle
  4. Storage: filesystems, mounts, LVM
  5. Networking: routes, iptables and nftables, DNS resolution order
  6. Users, permissions and capabilities
  7. Shell scripting good enough to automate your own work
Ends at LFCS Linux Foundation Certified System Administrator

Is it worth itPerformance-based like CKA, and the most transferable certification here because Linux does not churn. If you are strong on Kubernetes but vague on what the kernel is doing underneath, this closes the gap that eventually shows up in an incident.

Observability Engineer

Makes systems explain themselves before they fail.

You own the question "what is actually happening", and the harder one, "how would we have known sooner". Metrics, logs and traces are the tools, not the job.

  1. Why distributed systems need this at all
  2. Metrics, logs and traces, and what each is bad at
  3. Prometheus: the data model, scraping and PromQL
  4. Alerting that pages a human only when a human is needed
  5. OpenTelemetry as the vendor-neutral collection layer
  6. Tracing across service boundaries, and sampling honestly
  7. Dashboards people actually read during an incident
Ends at PCA Prometheus Certified Associate or OTCA, OpenTelemetry Certified Associate

Is it worth itBoth are multiple choice and neither will get you hired alone. They are worth it as a forcing function: PCA makes you learn PromQL properly, which most people fake for years. Take PCA if you own alerts, OTCA if you own instrumentation, and skip both if you already do this daily and nobody is asking you to prove it.

Platform Engineer

Builds the paved road other teams ship on.

Your users are other engineers. Success is measured in how rarely they need to understand any of this.

  1. Why interfaces exist, because you will be designing them
  2. CRDs and operators as the extension mechanism
  3. Golden paths, templates and self-service provisioning
  4. Multi-tenancy that holds under pressure
  5. Developer portals and service catalogues (Backstage)
  6. Cost and capacity as a platform concern, not an afterthought
Ends at CNPE Certified Cloud Native Platform Engineer

Is it worth itThe newest and most senior of these, and the only one aimed at design judgement rather than operation. Worth it once you are already doing platform work; it is a poor first certification because it assumes everything in the administrator path.

GitOps / Delivery Engineer

Makes deployment boring and reversible.

Git is the desired state; a controller makes reality match. Your job is that loop being trustworthy enough that people stop deploying by hand.

  1. The reconciliation loop, because GitOps is just this with Git as input
  2. Declarative manifests, Helm and Kustomize, and when each hurts
  3. Argo CD or Flux: drift detection and sync policy
  4. Progressive delivery: canaries and blue/green with real signals
  5. Secrets in a Git-driven world, done without leaking them
  6. Rollback you have actually rehearsed
Ends at CGOA Certified GitOps Associate or CAPA, Certified Argo Project Associate

Is it worth itCGOA covers the principles and is tool-agnostic; CAPA is specifically Argo. Both are associate-level multiple choice. If your shop already runs Argo, CAPA is the more immediately useful of the two, but neither is a hiring signal on the level of CKA.

Networking / Service Mesh

Owns how anything reaches anything else.

Connectivity, identity and traffic policy between services, plus the debugging that starts with "it works from my pod but not from theirs".

  1. How pods get addresses and reach each other
  2. Services, kube-proxy and what load balancing means here
  3. Getting traffic in: Ingress and the Gateway API
  4. Service mesh: mTLS, retries, timeouts and the sidecar cost
  5. eBPF as an alternative to iptables-based datapaths
  6. Debugging DNS, because it is usually DNS
Ends at ICA Istio Certified Associate or CCA, Cilium Certified Associate

Is it worth itBoth are tool-specific, so pick the one you actually run rather than the one that sounds better. If you run neither, learn the CNI layer properly first; a mesh certification on top of shaky networking fundamentals is a bad trade.

AI / Agent Infrastructure

The newest role here, and the least settled.

Running models and agents on the same infrastructure as everything else, plus the new problem of letting a model call your tools without handing it the keys.

  1. Everything in the developer path. This is not a shortcut around Kubernetes
  2. GPU scheduling, device plugins and why they are awkward
  3. Model serving, batching and autoscaling on expensive hardware
  4. MCP: how a model is given tools, and the trust boundary that creates
  5. Cost control, because this is where cloud bills go to die
Ends at MCPA Model Context Protocol Associate or PTCA, PyTorch Certified Associate

Is it worth itBrand new, launched September 2026, and the first certification against MCP. Beginner level and multiple choice, so treat it as evidence you have thought about the protocol and its trust model, not as a senior credential. Half the exam is runtime behaviour and security, which is the half people get wrong.

FinOps / Cloud Cost

The role that pays for itself, literally.

Making cloud spend a thing engineering decides deliberately rather than discovers monthly. Part engineering, part accounting, part diplomacy.

  1. Where the money actually goes: compute, egress, storage, idle
  2. Tagging and allocation good enough to answer "whose is this"
  3. Rightsizing and autoscaling as a cost lever, not just a reliability one
  4. Commitment discounts: reserved capacity and savings plans
  5. FOCUS: the standard that makes multi-cloud billing comparable
  6. Showback and chargeback without starting a civil war
Ends at FOCP FinOps Certified Practitioner

Is it worth itA different foundation and a different checkout from everything else on this page, so the Linux Foundation code does not apply to it. Genuinely useful if you are the person being asked why the bill went up, and close to useless if nobody has asked you that yet.

The completionist

Collecting the full set, deliberately.

Not a job. A recognition programme: hold all five Kubernetes certifications at once for Kubestronaut, or all sixteen for Golden Kubestronaut.

  1. KCNA and KCSA first: multiple choice, quickest wins
  2. CKA next, because CKS cannot be sat without an active one
  3. CKAD, while the kubectl muscle memory is fresh
  4. CKS last of the five
  5. Then the eleven others if you are going for Golden
  6. Watch renewal dates. All of them must be active simultaneously
Ends at Kubestronaut bundle KCNA + KCSA + CKA + CKAD + CKS or Golden Kubestronaut, All 16 certifications

Is it worth itBe honest with yourself about why. As a learning structure it is excellent, and the bundles cost meaningfully less than buying the exams one at a time. As a hiring signal, the fifth certification adds far less than the first did. If you already hold one or two, the upgrade bundles exist so you are not paying twice.

Certification links go to my own pages, which carry a partner discount code. I earn a commission if you buy through them, at no extra cost to you. That is also why the verdicts above say when to skip one: a roadmap where every road happens to end in something for sale is not a roadmap.
